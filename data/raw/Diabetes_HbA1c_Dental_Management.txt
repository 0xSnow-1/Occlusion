DIABETES AND ORAL HEALTH - CLINICAL PROTOCOL
HbA1c THRESHOLDS FOR ELECTIVE DENTAL & SURGICAL PROCEDURES

1. BACKGROUND

Diabetes mellitus and periodontal disease share a well-documented bidirectional
relationship. Poor glycemic control impairs neutrophil function and wound
healing and is associated with increased periodontal attachment loss; treating
periodontal disease can in turn modestly improve glycemic control (reported
HbA1c reductions of roughly 0.3-0.5% at 3-6 months post periodontal therapy
in some studies). HbA1c (glycated hemoglobin) reflects average blood glucose
over the preceding ~2-3 months and is the primary chairside risk-stratification
marker used before elective dental/surgical treatment planning.

2. HbA1c RISK STRATIFICATION TABLE

  HbA1c Range      Interpretation                 Elective Procedure Guidance
  ---------------  -----------------------------  --------------------------------
  < 6.5%           Non-diabetic / normal           No diabetes-related restriction
  6.5% - 6.9%       Well-controlled diabetes        Proceed with elective care as
                                                     planned; routine monitoring
  7.0% - 7.9%       Moderately controlled           Proceed with caution; consider
                                                     physician coordination for more
                                                     invasive procedures (implants,
                                                     extensive surgery); reinforce
                                                     oral hygiene and infection
                                                     control measures
  8.0% - 8.9%       Poorly controlled               Defer purely elective procedures
                                                     (e.g., elective implant placement)
                                                     until improved; urgent/emergent
                                                     dental treatment (abscess, pain,
                                                     infection) should NOT be delayed
  >= 9.0%           Very poorly controlled           Medical consultation strongly
                                                     recommended before any elective
                                                     surgical dental procedure; treat
                                                     urgent/emergent conditions with
                                                     antibiotic coverage and close
                                                     follow-up as needed regardless
                                                     of HbA1c

NOTE: An HbA1c threshold is a clinical reference point, not an absolute rule.
Some specialty literature (e.g., dental implant placement) cites a more
conservative <=7.0% (53 mmol/mol) target, with 8.0-8.5% commonly cited as
the point at which elective surgical deferral is generally advised. Always
weigh HbA1c alongside other comorbidities, medication compliance, and the
urgency of the presenting condition.

3. LINK TO PERIODONTITIS GRADING (2018 AAP/EFP CLASSIFICATION)

  Diabetic patients being staged/graded for periodontitis:
    - HbA1c < 7.0%  -> may be assigned periodontitis Grade B (moderate risk)
    - HbA1c >= 7.0% -> shifted to periodontitis Grade C (rapid progression /
                        high risk), regardless of radiographic progression rate

4. KEY PRINCIPLE: URGENT CARE IS NEVER WITHHELD FOR HbA1c ALONE

  Elevated HbA1c is NOT, by itself, a contraindication to necessary dental
  treatment. Symptomatic infection, abscess, or acute pain must be managed
  promptly regardless of glycemic control; delaying urgent/emergent care to
  "wait for better control" risks systemic spread of odontogenic infection.
  HbA1c informs elective-procedure TIMING and perioperative precautions, not
  whether urgent care is provided.

5. PERIOPERATIVE PRECAUTIONS FOR DIABETIC PATIENTS UNDERGOING DENTAL SURGERY

  - Schedule procedures in the morning when possible, after the patient has
    taken their usual diabetes medication and eaten a normal meal.
  - Confirm the patient has not skipped insulin/oral hypoglycemics due to
    fasting instructions intended for other procedures (dental surgery
    typically does not require fasting).
  - Have a source of fast-acting glucose available chairside in case of
    intraoperative hypoglycemia.
  - Consider antibiotic prophylaxis for more invasive procedures in patients
    with poorly controlled diabetes, per clinical judgment (this is distinct
    from cardiac IE prophylaxis regimens).
  - Reinforce postoperative wound care instructions; healing may be delayed
    in patients with HbA1c >= 8%.
